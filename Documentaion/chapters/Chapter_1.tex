\chapter{Introduction and Background}
\label{chap:introduction}

Viticulture, one of the world's most economically significant agricultural sectors, faces persistent challenges from foliar diseases that threaten grape quality and yield. Traditional vineyard monitoring relies on manual scouting by agronomists who visually inspect vines for disease symptoms—a process that is labor-intensive, subjective, and often detects infections too late for effective intervention~\cite{precision_agriculture_review}. The economic impact is substantial: major grape diseases such as Black Rot, Esca, and Leaf Blight can reduce yields by 30-50\% in severely affected vineyards, while preventative fungicide applications contribute to 20-30\% of operational costs and raise environmental concerns about pesticide residues in soil and groundwater.

The convergence of three technological advances—wireless sensor networks, low-power wide-area communication, and edge artificial intelligence—creates an opportunity to transform vineyard management through continuous, automated plant health monitoring. This thesis investigates whether sophisticated computer vision inference for disease detection can be integrated into ultra-low-power sensor networks while maintaining the energy efficiency required for solar-powered, multi-month autonomous operation. The fundamental challenge is reconciling the bandwidth and power demands of visual sensing with the stringent constraints of battery-powered agricultural IoT deployments.

Wireless sensor networks (WSN) have become a cornerstone technology for precision agriculture, providing spatially-distributed environmental monitoring across hectares of farmland without the infrastructure costs of wired systems~\cite{precision_agriculture_review, lorawan_agriculture}. Contemporary vineyard WSN deployments typically measure temperature, humidity, atmospheric pressure, and soil moisture at 10-30 minute intervals, transmitting concise telemetry packets (20-50 bytes) that enable years of operation on coin-cell batteries. These environmental measurements inform irrigation scheduling and disease risk models, but they provide only indirect indicators of plant health. When environmental conditions favor fungal infections (e.g., extended periods of high humidity and moderate temperatures), agronomists must still physically inspect vines to confirm whether disease symptoms have actually manifested—by which time treatment may be too late to prevent yield loss.

Visual sensing with cameras offers direct observation of plant health through analysis of leaf color, texture, and morphology. However, transmitting raw images—even compressed JPEG files of 100-500kB—is fundamentally incompatible with the power budgets of agricultural sensor nodes. A LoRaWAN radio consuming 120mA at 3.3V during transmission would require 15-75 seconds to send a single image at typical data rates of 0.3-5.5kbps, depleting a 2000mAh battery in days rather than months. Cellular connectivity (LTE-M, NB-IoT) offers higher bandwidth but consumes comparable energy per bit and incurs subscription fees that become prohibitive for dense sensor deployments with hundreds of nodes per vineyard.

Edge AI computing—deploying deep learning inference directly on sensor nodes—resolves this bandwidth-power dilemma by transmitting only inference results rather than raw imagery. A disease classification result such as "Leaf Blight detected with 92\% confidence" requires merely 10-20 bytes, orders of magnitude less than the source image, while still providing actionable plant health diagnostics. This approach has been enabled by two recent developments: efficient neural network architectures such as MobileNets~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} and YOLO~\cite{ultralytics2024yolo11} that achieve high accuracy with 10-100× fewer parameters than earlier CNN models, and microcontrollers with hardware acceleration features (SIMD vector instructions, neural processing units) that execute inference at practical speeds despite consuming only milliwatts of power~\cite{edge_ai_survey}.

The ESP32-S3 microcontroller exemplifies this new generation of edge AI-capable platforms. Its dual-core Xtensa LX7 processors running at 240MHz include vector extensions that accelerate the multiply-accumulate operations central to convolutional neural networks, while 512KB of internal SRAM and 8MB of external PSRAM provide sufficient memory for model weights and intermediate activations. Espressif's ESP-DL framework supplies optimized kernels that exploit these architectural features, enabling inference at 5-15 frames per second for INT8-quantized models. When combined with deep sleep modes consuming only 8$\mu$W between inferences, the ESP32-S3 can operate on battery or solar-harvested power for extended periods.

Deep learning models for plant disease classification have matured considerably over the past five years. Convolutional neural networks trained on publicly available datasets such as PlantVillage~\cite{hughes2015open}—which contains 54,000+ labeled images of healthy and diseased leaves across 14 crop species—routinely achieve 95-99\% accuracy on held-out test sets~\cite{grape_disease_detection}. However, most published research evaluates models on desktop GPUs or embedded Linux platforms (NVIDIA Jetson, Raspberry Pi), with limited attention to the resource constraints of microcontroller-class processors. The typical deployment pipeline combines object detection to localize individual leaves within images, followed by classification to diagnose disease status. YOLO (You Only Look Once) architectures enable real-time detection through single-pass inference, but standard YOLO models require 20-60MB of memory and billions of floating-point operations per frame—far exceeding microcontroller capabilities. Fortunately, model compression techniques including pruning, quantization, and neural architecture search have produced lightweight variants such as YOLO-Nano and ESPDet-Pico that sacrifice 5-10 percentage points of accuracy for 10-100× reductions in computational requirements.

Long Range Wide Area Network (LoRaWAN) has emerged as the dominant communication protocol for agricultural IoT due to its favorable balance of range, power consumption, and cost~\cite{lorawan_specification, mekki2019comparative, sinha2017survey}. Operating in unlicensed sub-GHz ISM bands (868MHz in Europe, 915MHz in North America), LoRaWAN achieves 2-15km line-of-sight range with transmission power of only 14-20dBm, allowing a single gateway to cover 50-200 hectares of farmland. The star network topology, where end nodes communicate directly with a central gateway that bridges to IP networks, simplifies deployment compared to mesh protocols that require nodes to route traffic through multi-hop paths. LoRaWAN Class A devices achieve ultra-low power consumption through duty-cycled operation: nodes transmit brief uplink messages and open short receive windows for potential downlink responses, spending the vast majority of time in sleep modes consuming microamps.

The protocol is well-suited for environmental telemetry that tolerates latency (minutes to hours), but maximum transmission units of 51-222 bytes (depending on spreading factor) and 1\% regulatory duty cycle restrictions constrain payload size and frequency. An edge AI disease monitoring system must therefore encode classification results, confidence scores, and metadata within these strict limits while maintaining sufficient diagnostic richness for agronomic decision-making. This requires careful payload design that prioritizes actionable information: rather than transmitting per-leaf classifications for every detected leaf, the system can aggregate predictions into disease distribution histograms (e.g., "7 leaves observed: 5 healthy, 2 leaf blight") that convey plant-level health status in 16-20 bytes.

This thesis addresses the research question: \textit{can sophisticated computer vision inference for grape leaf disease detection be integrated into ultra-low-power LoRaWAN sensor networks to enable visual plant health monitoring while maintaining multi-month autonomous operation on solar-harvested energy?} The investigation encompasses model development and optimization, edge AI sensor implementation, system integration with existing LoRaWAN infrastructure, energy characterization, and field deployment validation. The primary contributions include: (1) design and validation of an integrated edge AI visual monitoring system combining YOLO detection, CNN classification, and LoRaWAN communication on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller that achieves 99.75\% classification accuracy while consuming 0.815mW average power; (2) development of a hybrid count-weighted aggregation strategy that improves plant-level diagnosis accuracy by 3.2 percentage points over single-leaf inference; (3) demonstration that solar energy harvesting with a 6W panel sustains edge AI inference workloads, maintaining 13-37× energy surplus across seasons and 99.8\% uptime during six-month field deployment; (4) design of a 16-byte LoRaWAN payload structure efficiently encoding disease classifications, confidence metrics, and system telemetry; and (5) empirical characterization of performance tradeoffs, power budgets, and environmental robustness for microcontroller-based edge AI in outdoor agricultural environments.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 surveys related work in wireless sensor networks for agriculture, plant disease detection using deep learning, edge AI on microcontrollers, and LoRaWAN applications, identifying gaps that motivate this research. Chapter 3 describes the system architecture including model training and optimization, ESP32-S3 deployment, camera integration, dual-model inference pipeline, LoRaWAN payload design, and integration with existing network infrastructure (The Things Stack, MQTT, MongoDB, Grafana). Chapter 4 presents experimental evaluation analyzing inference performance, aggregation strategy effectiveness, communication characteristics, and duty-cycled power consumption through controlled benchmarks and six-month field deployment in a Mediterranean vineyard. Chapter 5 concludes with a summary of findings, discussion of limitations, and directions for future work including domain adaptation, multi-modal sensing, federated learning, and integration with decision support systems for precision agriculture.
