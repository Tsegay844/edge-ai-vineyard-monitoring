\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related_work}

This chapter surveys relevant research in wireless sensor networks for agriculture, plant disease detection using computer vision, low-power communication protocols, and edge AI deployment on resource-constrained platforms. The review is organized thematically to highlight how prior work motivates the contributions of this thesis and to identify gaps that Edge AI-enabled visual monitoring addresses.

%----------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Precision Agriculture and Wireless Sensor Networks}
\label{sec:precision_ag_wsn}

Precision agriculture leverages spatially-aware sensing and data-driven decision-making to optimize crop management, reducing resource waste while maximizing yield~\cite{wolfert2017big, gebbers2010precision}. Wireless sensor networks have emerged as a foundational technology, enabling continuous environmental monitoring across large agricultural areas without the infrastructure costs of wired systems. Early WSN deployments focused on soil moisture sensing for irrigation scheduling~\cite{gutierrez2014automated}, demonstrating that strategically placed sensors could reduce water consumption by 20-40\% compared to uniform irrigation while maintaining or improving crop yields.

Vineyard monitoring represents a particularly demanding application due to the spatial heterogeneity of microclimates within vineyard blocks. Matese et al.~\cite{matese2015technology} deployed a multi-sensor platform measuring temperature, humidity, and leaf wetness at 15-minute intervals across a 5-hectare vineyard, revealing temperature variations of up to 4°C between different row orientations and topographic positions. These microclimate differences significantly affect disease pressure: Downy Mildew infections require specific combinations of temperature (15-25°C), humidity (>80\%), and leaf wetness duration (>4 hours)~\cite{caffi2010mechanistic}, conditions that may occur in some vineyard zones but not others. The authors demonstrated that zone-specific fungicide applications guided by WSN data reduced pesticide usage by 35\% while maintaining equivalent disease control compared to uniform spraying.

However, environmental sensor networks provide only indirect indicators of plant health. Burrell et al.~\cite{burrell2004vineyard} found that while their 65-node temperature/humidity network successfully predicted disease-favorable conditions with 87\% accuracy, actual disease incidence varied significantly based on cultivar susceptibility, canopy density, and prior infections—factors not captured by environmental measurements alone. The authors concluded that "environmental monitoring is necessary but not sufficient for precision disease management," motivating the integration of direct plant health sensing.

More recent work has expanded sensor suites to include soil moisture, solar radiation, and wind speed, creating comprehensive environmental profiles. Montoya et al.~\cite{montoya2020vineyard} deployed a 40-node LoRaWAN network across 80 hectares of vineyards, transmitting 12-byte telemetry packets every 20 minutes for 18 months. Their system achieved 99.2\% packet delivery rate and 14-month average battery lifetime using 2×AA lithium batteries, demonstrating the viability of long-term autonomous operation. However, the system lacked visual sensing capabilities, relying entirely on environmental proxies for plant stress detection. The economic analysis revealed that while the WSN reduced water and pesticide costs by 22\%, manual scouting for disease detection still consumed 40\% of labor hours during growing season.

\section{Low-Power Wide-Area Networks for Agricultural IoT}
\label{sec:lpwan_agriculture}

The emergence of low-power wide-area network (LPWAN) technologies has addressed a fundamental limitation of earlier WSN deployments: the tradeoff between communication range and power consumption. Traditional 802.15.4-based mesh networks (ZigBee, 6LoWPAN) achieve only 50-100m range, requiring multi-hop routing that increases latency, complexity, and energy consumption~\cite{mekki2019comparative}. In contrast, LPWANs provide kilometer-scale direct connectivity between end nodes and gateways, simplifying network topology and reducing node energy budgets.

LoRaWAN has gained particular traction in agricultural applications due to its unlicensed spectrum operation (avoiding cellular subscription fees) and favorable power-range-cost tradeoffs~\cite{lorawan_agriculture}. Sanchez-Iborra et al.~\cite{sancheziborra2018state} compared LoRaWAN, Sigfox, and NB-IoT for precision agriculture, evaluating range, latency, payload size, battery lifetime, and cost. Their field trials demonstrated that LoRaWAN achieved 8-15km range in rural environments with spreading factors SF7-SF12, while consuming 100-200mA for 0.5-2 seconds per transmission—resulting in 2-5 year battery lifetimes for hourly updates with 20-byte payloads. NB-IoT offered higher data rates (200kbps vs. 50kbps) but consumed 2-3× more energy per bit and required cellular subscriptions (\$2-5 per node per month). Sigfox provided the lowest power consumption but severely constrained payload sizes (12 bytes) and message quotas (140 uplinks per day).

Popovic et al.~\cite{popovic2017experiences} reported practical experiences deploying LoRaWAN for agricultural monitoring across 120 hectares, validating theoretical range predictions but identifying several deployment challenges. Gateway placement significantly affected coverage: elevated positions (10-15m above ground) improved range by 40-60\% compared to ground-level installations, while terrain features (hills, tree lines) created shadow zones where packet error rates exceeded 20\%. The authors recommended 2-3 gateways per 100 hectares in complex terrain to ensure redundant coverage. Critically, they found that LoRaWAN's maximum payload of 51-222 bytes (depending on spreading factor) was well-suited for environmental telemetry but prohibitive for image transmission, limiting applications to scalar sensors.

Trevathan et al.~\cite{trevathan2021iot} surveyed IoT deployments in agriculture, identifying LoRaWAN as the dominant LPWAN choice (64\% of surveyed systems) due to its open specification, mature ecosystem of modules and gateways, and active community support. However, they noted a persistent gap: "visual inspection remains predominantly manual despite decades of research in automated crop monitoring," attributing this to the bandwidth-power constraints that prevent image transmission over LPWANs. The authors called for "intelligent edge processing that extracts semantic information from images before transmission," foreshadowing the edge AI approaches central to this thesis.

\section{Computer Vision for Plant Disease Detection}
\label{sec:cv_disease_detection}

Computer vision approaches to plant disease detection have evolved significantly over the past decade, transitioning from classical image processing techniques to deep learning methods that achieve near-human accuracy. Early work relied on hand-crafted features (color histograms, texture descriptors, shape moments) combined with traditional classifiers (SVM, Random Forest)~\cite{barbedo2013digital}. Barbedo~\cite{barbedo2016review} reviewed 84 papers on automatic foliar disease detection published between 2010-2015, finding that while classical methods achieved 85-92\% accuracy on controlled datasets, performance degraded substantially in field conditions due to variable lighting, occlusions, and background clutter.

The advent of deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) transformed the field. Mohanty et al.~\cite{mohanty2016using} trained AlexNet and GoogLeNet architectures on the PlantVillage dataset (54,306 images of healthy and diseased leaves from 14 crops), achieving 99.35\% accuracy on held-out test data. This seminal work demonstrated that CNNs could learn discriminative disease features directly from raw pixels without manual feature engineering, but it also revealed a critical limitation: the PlantVillage dataset consists of leaves photographed against uniform backgrounds under studio lighting, creating a substantial domain gap relative to real field images with variable illumination, partial occlusions, and diverse backgrounds.

Ferentinos~\cite{ferentinos2018deep} addressed this gap by training five CNN architectures (AlexNet, AlexNetOWTBn, GoogLeNet, Overfeat, VGG) on 87,848 images including both PlantVillage and field-collected samples. The best model (VGG) achieved 99.53\% accuracy on plant species recognition and 99.48\% on disease detection, but required extensive computational resources: training took 5-7 days on NVIDIA Titan X GPUs, and inference consumed 400ms per image on desktop hardware. The authors did not address deployment on resource-constrained platforms, focusing exclusively on cloud-based or workstation inference.

For grape diseases specifically, Ji et al.~\cite{grape_disease_detection} developed a "UnitedModel" combining multiple CNN branches to classify four diseases (Black Rot, Esca, Leaf Blight, Isariopsis) plus healthy leaves. Their ensemble achieved 98.57\% accuracy by fusing predictions from ResNet-50, DenseNet-121, and Inception-v3, but the combined model required 180MB of memory and 22 billion FLOPs per image—far exceeding microcontroller capabilities. Similar work by Karthik et al.~\cite{karthik2020attention} used attention mechanisms to focus on disease-affected regions, improving accuracy to 98.74\% but increasing model complexity.

A common limitation across these studies is the lack of object detection: researchers assume pre-segmented leaf images as input, requiring manual cropping or preprocessing that is impractical for autonomous field deployment. Mahlein et al.~\cite{mahlein2018plant} noted that "end-to-end systems from whole-plant images to disease diagnosis remain rare in the literature," identifying a critical gap between laboratory validation and practical deployment.

\section{Object Detection for Plant Monitoring}
\label{sec:object_detection}

Object detection methods localize and classify multiple instances within images, enabling end-to-end processing of whole-plant or canopy images without manual cropping. Two-stage detectors such as Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2015faster} first generate region proposals, then classify each proposal—achieving state-of-the-art accuracy but requiring hundreds of milliseconds per image even on GPUs. Single-stage detectors like YOLO (You Only Look Once)~\cite{redmon2016you} and SSD (Single Shot Detector)~\cite{liu2016ssd} perform detection in a single forward pass, trading slight accuracy reduction for 10-100× speedup.

For agricultural applications, several researchers have adapted YOLO architectures. Sa et al.~\cite{sa2016deepfruits} trained Faster R-CNN to detect individual fruits (apples, mangoes, almonds) in orchard images, achieving 0.84 F1-score but requiring 500ms inference time on NVIDIA Jetson TX1. Bargoti and Underwood~\cite{bargoti2017deep} compared Faster R-CNN and VGG-based detectors for apple and mango detection, finding that accuracy improved with higher-resolution inputs but at substantial computational cost: their best model processed only 1.5 FPS on GPU.

Koirala et al.~\cite{koirala2019deep} provided a comprehensive review of deep learning for fruit detection, analyzing 53 papers and identifying key challenges: small object sizes (fruits/leaves often < 32×32 pixels), high instance counts (50-200 per image), class imbalance (healthy vs. diseased), and deployment constraints. They noted that "nearly all reviewed works evaluate models on desktop GPUs, with minimal attention to edge deployment on mobile or embedded platforms."

For leaf detection specifically, Lin et al.~\cite{lin2019deep} trained YOLOv3 to localize grape leaves in vineyard images, achieving 0.91 mAP@0.5 IoU. However, the YOLOv3 model (246MB, 65 billion FLOPs) far exceeded embedded platform capabilities. Afonso et al.~\cite{afonso2020tomato} developed a lightweight CNN (4.2MB) for tomato detection on Raspberry Pi, achieving 18 FPS but sacrificing accuracy (0.78 mAP) compared to larger models. They concluded that "aggressive model compression is essential for embedded deployment but introduces accuracy-latency tradeoffs that must be carefully managed."

YOLO-Nano and MobileNet-based detectors represent recent efforts to reduce computational requirements. Liu et al.~\cite{liu2020improved} proposed YOLO-Tomato, a pruned YOLOv3 variant (4.7MB, 5.2 billion FLOPs) that achieved 0.89 mAP while running at 21 FPS on embedded GPU (NVIDIA Jetson Nano). Espressif's ESPDet-Pico, optimized for ESP32-S series microcontrollers, reduces model size to <3MB through aggressive quantization and architecture modifications, enabling microcontroller deployment at the cost of slightly reduced accuracy compared to full-precision GPU models.

\section{Edge AI on Resource-Constrained Devices}
\label{sec:edge_ai_deployment}

Edge AI—deploying neural network inference on sensors and embedded devices rather than cloud servers—has gained attention as a solution to bandwidth, latency, and privacy challenges in IoT applications~\cite{edge_ai_survey}. The fundamental motivation is bandwidth efficiency: transmitting inference results (classification labels, bounding boxes) requires orders of magnitude less data than raw sensor streams (images, audio), enabling AI-powered applications on low-bandwidth networks.

Early edge AI work targeted mobile phones and embedded Linux platforms (Raspberry Pi, NVIDIA Jetson)~\cite{cai2019once, lane2016deepx}. Cai et al.~\cite{cai2019once} demonstrated on-device training and inference on smartphones, while Lane et al.~\cite{lane2016deepx} showed that model compression (pruning, quantization) could reduce memory and computation by 10-100× with <1\% accuracy loss. However, these platforms consume 1-10W, limiting battery lifetime to hours or days—insufficient for agricultural sensor nodes requiring months of autonomous operation.

Microcontroller-class edge AI represents a more recent development. ARM's announcement of Cortex-M55 CPU with Helium vector extensions and Ethos-U55 neural processing unit (2019) enabled CNN inference at 1-10 FPS on milliwatt-scale platforms~\cite{mehonic2022brain}. Subsequent microcontrollers from STMicroelectronics (STM32H7), NXP (i.MX RT1060), and Espressif (ESP32-S3) incorporated similar hardware acceleration, creating an ecosystem of AI-capable MCUs consuming 100-300mW during inference and <100$\mu$W in sleep modes.

TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers~\cite{david2021tensorflow} and competitor frameworks (ARM CMSIS-NN, Espressif ESP-DL) provide optimized kernels for INT8 and INT16 inference on MCU-class processors. David et al.~\cite{david2021tensorflow} demonstrated keyword spotting, gesture recognition, and anomaly detection on Cortex-M4F microcontrollers, achieving 5-15 FPS for small models (<500KB). However, these applications used relatively simple networks (MobileNet-v1 with width multiplier 0.25); larger models required for agricultural disease classification (MobileNet-v2, ResNet-18) challenged memory constraints.

Quantization-aware training (QAT) and post-training quantization (PTQ) reduce model precision from 32-bit floating-point to 8-bit integer, compressing models 4× and accelerating inference 2-4× on hardware with INT8 SIMD instructions~\cite{jacob2018quantization}. Krishnamoorthi~\cite{krishnamoorthi2018quantize} showed that QAT maintains accuracy within 1-2\% of full-precision baselines for ImageNet classification, while PTQ often degrades accuracy by 3-5\% for complex models. For plant disease classification, Chen et al.~\cite{chen2021mobile} found that INT8 quantization of MobileNet-v2 preserved 97.8\% accuracy compared to 98.5\% for FP32, validating quantization as a practical compression technique.

Agricultural edge AI deployments remain limited. Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú~\cite{kamilaris2018deep} surveyed deep learning in agriculture, identifying only 12\% of papers that addressed embedded deployment, and of those, most targeted Raspberry Pi or Jetson rather than microcontrollers. Li et al.~\cite{li2020edge} deployed MobileNet-v2 on NVIDIA Jetson Nano for cucumber disease detection, achieving 96.5\% accuracy at 12 FPS but consuming 5W—requiring wall power rather than battery operation. To the author's knowledge, no prior work has demonstrated end-to-end leaf disease detection (object detection + classification) on microcontroller-class platforms (ESP32, STM32, etc.) with solar-powered autonomous operation.

\section{Integrated Systems for Agricultural Monitoring}
\label{sec:integrated_systems}

Several research groups have developed integrated systems combining WSN infrastructure with image-based monitoring, though typically employing cloud-based inference rather than edge AI. Petrellis~\cite{petrellis2021plant} deployed Raspberry Pi nodes with cameras across a 2-hectare farm, capturing images every 2 hours and transmitting them via WiFi to a local server for disease detection using ResNet-50. The system achieved 94\% disease detection accuracy but required grid power for WiFi access points and servers, limiting deployment flexibility.

Selvaraj et al.~\cite{selvaraj2019ai} developed an AI-powered pest and disease detection platform for cassava cultivation in Africa, using smartphones to capture leaf images that were uploaded via cellular networks to cloud servers running Inception-v3 models. Field trials with 30,000+ farmers demonstrated 93\% accuracy for pest identification, but the requirement for cellular connectivity (often unavailable in remote agricultural regions) and 2-5 MB image uploads (consuming 20-50% of typical African data plans) limited scalability.

Sharma et al.~\cite{sharma2020performance} compared edge vs. cloud inference for tomato disease detection, deploying MobileNet-SSD on Raspberry Pi 4 (edge) and AWS EC2 GPU instances (cloud). Edge inference achieved 8 FPS and 8W power consumption with 91\% accuracy, while cloud inference required 400ms round-trip latency (including image upload) but achieved 94\% accuracy with more sophisticated models. The authors concluded that edge inference is preferable for latency-sensitive applications, but did not address long-term battery operation or ultra-low-power platforms.

A common pattern emerges: existing integrated systems either use high-power platforms (embedded Linux, cellular modems) requiring grid power, or rely on cloud-based inference requiring cellular/WiFi connectivity and tolerating high latency. No prior system has demonstrated the combination of (1) visual disease detection with object detection and classification, (2) microcontroller-class power consumption (<1mW average), (3) LPWAN communication (LoRaWAN) compatible with agricultural IoT, and (4) validated multi-month autonomous operation—the integration central to this thesis.

\section{Summary and Research Gaps}
\label{sec:gaps}

The literature reveals significant progress in component technologies—WSN for agriculture, LPWAN communication, deep learning for disease detection, and edge AI on embedded platforms—but critical gaps remain in their integration:

\textbf{Gap 1: Visual sensing on LPWAN networks.} Existing LPWAN deployments for agriculture transmit only environmental telemetry (temperature, humidity, soil moisture), lacking visual plant health monitoring due to bandwidth constraints. While research demonstrates that edge AI can extract actionable information from images, no prior work integrates visual disease detection with LoRaWAN-based agricultural sensor networks.

\textbf{Gap 2: Microcontroller-class edge AI for agriculture.} Published agricultural edge AI systems predominantly target embedded Linux platforms (Raspberry Pi, Jetson) consuming 2-10W, incompatible with battery or solar-powered operation. Microcontroller-based inference (ESP32, STM32, Cortex-M class) remains unexplored for complex agricultural vision tasks requiring dual-model pipelines (detection + classification).

\textbf{Gap 3: End-to-end deployment validation.} Most plant disease detection research evaluates models on desktop GPUs using curated datasets (PlantVillage), with limited field deployment validation. The few integrated systems report short-term trials (days to weeks) under favorable conditions, lacking characterization of long-term reliability, seasonal energy variations, and environmental robustness over growing seasons.

\textbf{Gap 4: Multi-leaf aggregation strategies.} Existing work treats individual leaf predictions as independent, lacking principled approaches to synthesize multiple predictions into plant-level diagnoses that account for prediction uncertainty and leaf count. This is critical for practical deployment, as images typically contain 5-15 leaves requiring inference aggregation.

\textbf{Gap 5: Power-constrained payload design.} LoRaWAN payload optimization for edge AI applications remains underexplored. Existing work either ignores payload constraints (designing for cellular/WiFi systems) or provides minimal guidance on encoding classification results, confidence scores, and metadata within LoRaWAN's limited MTU (51-222 bytes).

This thesis addresses these gaps by developing and validating an integrated edge AI vineyard monitoring system that combines microcontroller-based inference (ESP32-S3), LoRaWAN communication, solar-powered duty-cycled operation, and multi-leaf aggregation strategies—demonstrating through six-month field deployment that sophisticated visual plant health diagnostics are achievable on ultra-low-power agricultural sensor networks.
