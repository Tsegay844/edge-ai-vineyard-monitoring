\section{IoT \& LoRaWAN Vineyard Monitoring Systems}
\label{sec:iot_lorawan_vineyard}
The traditional vineyard monitoring systems are focused on environmental data acquisition through wireless sensor networks. Hnatiuc et al. developed an intelligent grapevine disease detection using IoT sensor network that integrates environmental sensors for monitoring temperature, humidity, and atmospheric conditions~\cite{hnatiuc2023intelligent}. This approach represents the foundational methodology where IoT sensors collect environmental data to predict plant health status. Next, Gawande et al. proposed early prediction of grape disease occurrence using a hybrid classifier model in association with IoT sensors, demonstrating the integration of machine learning with IoT for environmental monitoring~\cite{gawande2024early}. Their work emphasized that monitoring environmental parameters via wireless sensor networks enables the prediction of plant diseases~\cite{gawande2025analyzing}.

The adoption of LoRaWAN technology became emerged advancement for large-scale vineyard monitoring due to its nature in long-range, low-power characteristics. Kirubakaran and Samreen proposed a Grape Leaf Diseases Identification System Using Convolutional Neural Networks and LoRa Technology, which is an early attempt to combine the CNN diseases detection with LoRa network communication~\cite{kirubakaran2024grape}. More recently, Hnatiuc et al. made a comprehensive study on IoT monitoring for early identification of diseases in grapevines, integrating AI with IoT sensor networks~\cite{hnatiuc2025aiot}. Their study reveled how the integration of AI with IoT sensor networks can enhance the diseases detection in grapevines, but it was primarily focused on environmental data collection and did not explore the edge-based visual-based monitoring.

\section{Deep Learning Models for Grape Leaf Disease Detection}
\label{sec:deep_learning_grape_leaf_disease}


The usage of deep learning techniques in grape leaf disease detection brought some improvemet, evolving from the traditional machine learning approaches into sophisticated Neural Network architectures. Aher et al. provided comprehensive  review on deep learning for grape leaf disease detection\cite{aher2025deep}, by putting similar methods together and identifying key challenges in that domain. then early approaches were employed using the classical machine learning techniques, as demonstrated by Shantkumari and Uma, who used machine learning techniques for detection of grape leaf disease using SVM and IKNN \cite{shantkumari2023machine}. Javidan et al. further explored diagnosis of grape leaf diseases using automatic K-means clustering and machine learning~\cite{javidan2023diagnosis}, achieved reasonable accuracy but with extensive manual feature engineering.

The transition to CNN showed a significant change in detection precision and automation. Diana Andrushia et al. introduced image-based disease classification on grape leaves using convolutional capsule network architectures\cite{diana2023image}, achieving accuracy of 99.12\%. Nargotra and Sharma demonstrated grape leaf disease detection using deep learning with CNN-based approaches\cite{nargotra2023grape}, while Soni explored deep learning for accurate grape disease classification in ResNet50 architecture\cite{soni2024deep}. The evolution towards to the sophisticated architectures is evidence on Praveen et al.'s work on the novel classification approach for grape leaf disease detection based on different attention deep learning techniques\cite{praveen2023novel}, which applied attention mechanism    to enhance feature decriptions.

Since the transfer learning became the dominant strategy to deal with not enough traning data problems. Vo, Kheo and Nhon et al. together they proposed fusing Xception and ResNet50 features for robust grape leaf disease classification\cite{vo2025fusing}, demonstrating that combining several pre-trained architectures yields higher performance. Yadav et al. used the using VGG16 \& Inception V3 models to investigate enhanced disease detection prediction accuracy of grape leaves\cite{yadav2025enhancing}, comparing deep learning architectures for classification tasks. Khursheed and Kaur presented improved grape leaf disease detection with GAN-augmented ResNet and EfficientNet~\cite{khursheed2025improved}, utilizing generative adversarial networks for data augmentation to address dataset imbalance.


%recent ------
The recent advancement have been focused on transformer-based architectures and hybrid ones. Zhang et al. developed a grape leaf disease recognition using the transformers \cite{zhang2025lightweight}, shows how capable transformer designs are architectures for disease recognition. Li et al. also came with the grape disease detection system using transformer's vision and environmental sensing\cite{li2025grape}, representing a novel approach that fuses visual and environmental data streams. Karthik et al. suggested the idea of GrapeLeafNet which is a dual-track feature fusion network using inception-ResNet and shuffle-transformer for accurate grape leaf disease identification\cite{karthik2024grapeleafnet}, achieving high accuracy scores because of its dual-track feature combination. Another further contribution done by Zhang et al. the cross-resolution transformer for grape leaf diseases segmentaion using context mining\cite{zhang2023crformer}, demonstrating that cross-resolution transformer architecture design enables the precise disease segmentation.


There are some special architectures made for specific detection challenges. Talaat et al. Introduced a DeepLeaf, an optimized deep learning approach for automated recognition of grapevine leaf diseases\cite{talaat2025deepleaf}, which optimize neural architectures for multi-class disease classification. Combining deep learning with manually created features, Atesoglu and Bingol suggested the detection and classification of grape leaf diseases with enhanced hybrid model based on feature engineering\cite{atesoglu2025detection}.Combining deep feature extraction with traditional classifiers in hybrid techniques can produce competitive performance, as demonestrated by Ishengoma and Lyimo's ensemble model for grape leaf disease detection using CNN feature extractors and random forest classifier\cite{ishengoma2024ensemble}. Using grape leaf disease identification with sparse data via GAN and CNN, Chen and Wu addressed data scarcity by means of GAN-based data augmentation to achieve the precision agriculture metrics\cite{chen2023grape}.

The integrating object detection frameworks have made it possible to simultaneously localize and classify plant diseases in complex field environments. The YOLOv7 was used by Yang et al. to study the detection of small lesions on grape leaves \cite{yang2024detection}, and he addressed the difficulty spotting the early-stage disease symptoms. Through architectural modifications, Chen et al. developed an enhanced lightweight grape leaf disease detection method \cite{chen2024yolov8}, therefore improving it performance. Ji et al. carried out a research on grape leaf disease detection method based on NMA-YOLOv8n by introducing a novel attention mechanisms to YOLO architectures\cite{ji2025research}. Particularly in relation to boundary detection issues in the situations of dense foliage, Zhou and Yu suggested a lightweight grape leaf disease identification for complex field scenes–YOLO-Grape model and its boundary-lesion coalesce optimization policies\cite{zhou2025lightweight}.



The next focus has been given to domain-specific challenges including multi-disease detection and disease severity evaluation. Mehta et al.  investigated the grape leaf disease severity analysis using federated learning with CNN techniques\cite{mehta2023grape},  hence bringing privacy-preserving distributed learning for disease assessment.Combining spatial and temporal data for dynamic disease monitoring, Gaur et al. worked on fighting grape black rot with deep learning using CNN-LSTM hybrid model for disease severity classification\cite{gaur2023fighting}. In a review of advancements in deep learning for accurate classification of grape leaves and diagnosis of grape diseases\cite{kunduracioglu2024advancements},Kunduracioglu and Pacal chronicled the change from binary classification to multi-class, multi-disease detection systems. Many other contributions include work by Naik et al. on disease detection in grape leaves\cite{naik2025disease}, Shetty and Shetty on grape leaf disease detection using deep learning approach"\cite{shetty2025grape}, and Liu and Li on grape leaf disease classification based on convolutional neural networks"\cite{liu2025grape}. These all contributions represent the ongoing evolution of deep learning techniques for grape leaf disease detection, with a clear trend towards more sophisticated architectures, multi-disease detection capabilities and consideration of real-world complexities.

\section{Research Gaps on the Existing LoRaWAN-based Vineyard Monitoring System}
\label{sec:precision_ag_wsn}
%Para 1: IoT-LoRaWAN Gap (Environmental vs. Visual)
Despite substantial progress in both IoT-based vineyard monitoring and deep learning for disease detection, a critical gap persists in the integration of these domains for practical, real-time disease detection at the network edge. Existing LoRaWAN-based vineyard monitoring systems, as demonstrated by Hnatiuc et al.\cite{hnatiuc2023intelligent}, Gawande et al.\cite{gawande2024early}, and Almufareh et al.~\cite{almufareh2024intelligent}, have primarily focused on environmental parameter sensing. While these systems successfully transmit temperature, humidity, and atmospheric data over LoRaWAN networks, they lack the capability to directly assess plant health through visual inspection of leaf pathology. The fundamental limitation is that environmental monitoring provides indirect, correlational indicators of disease risk rather than direct, real-time disease detection through image analysis.

%Para 2: Deep Learning Gap (Computational Constraints)
Conversely, the substantial body of work on deep learning for grape leaf disease detection, including sophisticated architectures such as those proposed by Karthik et al.\cite{karthik2024grapeleafnet}, Zhang et al.\cite{zhang2023crformer}, and Talaat et al.~\cite{talaat2025deepleaf}, has predominantly assumed unconstrained computational environments with access to high-performance GPUs and abundant network bandwidth. These approaches achieve impressive accuracy—often exceeding 95\% for multi-class disease classification—but their deployment requirements are incompatible with the resource constraints of field-deployed sensor nodes. The computational demands of state-of-the-art models, typically requiring hundreds of megabytes of memory and billions of floating-point operations per inference, exceed the capabilities of microcontroller-class edge devices by several orders of magnitude. This incompatibility prevents the deployment of advanced disease detection capabilities in remote vineyard locations where grid power and high-bandwidth connectivity are unavailable.


%Para 3: Lightweight Edge Limitations (Platform)
The emerging work on lightweight and edge-based disease detection, exemplified by Karim et al.\cite{karim2024enhancing}, Lazcano-García et al.\cite{lazcano2025deep}, and Deepa et al.~\cite{deepa2026net}, has made significant strides toward edge deployment. However, these implementations have primarily targeted single-board computers such as Raspberry Pi and NVIDIA Jetson, or smartphone platforms, which offer substantially greater computational resources than the microcontroller-class devices typically employed in wireless sensor networks. The power consumption profiles of these platforms—often several watts during active inference—combined with their inability to operate within the strict bandwidth constraints of LoRaWAN networks (typically 51-222 bytes per payload depending on data rate and regional parameters), render them unsuitable for battery-powered, long-term deployment scenarios characteristic of agricultural monitoring networks.


%Para 4: General End-to-End Integration Gap
The development of complete, end-to-end systems that integrate image acquisition, object detection, disease classification, and result aggregation within a single microcontroller-class device operating under LoRaWAN network constraints remains particularly underexplored. Kirubakaran and Samreen~\cite{kirubakaran2024grape} proposed combining CNNs with LoRa technology, but their implementation performed classification on pre-cropped leaf images and did not address the full pipeline of leaf detection within complex field scenes. Their approach assumed that leaf segmentation would be performed externally, thereby avoiding the computational challenge of integrated object detection and classification on resource-constrained hardware.

%Para 5: Recent Integration Attempts
Recent attempts to bridge this gap have made progress but remain insufficient for microcontroller-class deployment. Tao et al.\cite{tao2025icrop} developed iCrop+, integrating TinyML-based image segmentation with LoRa communication for crop disease monitoring. However, their implementation performed segmentation and CNN inference on Raspberry Pi rather than microcontroller-class devices, and did not address the specific challenges of grape leaf disease detection or result aggregation from multiple leaves within a single frame. Mamun et al.\cite{mamun2025grape} developed "Grape Guard: a YOLO-based mobile application for detecting grape leaf diseases," but their mobile application approach assumes smartphone-class computational resources and cellular network connectivity, neither of which are available in typical vineyard sensor deployments. These works demonstrate the feasibility of individual components but do not achieve the integration required for practical, autonomous vineyard monitoring on ultra-low-power platforms.

%Para 6: Detection-Classification Integration
Beyond the broader system integration challenges, the technical integration of object detection and classification within a unified edge pipeline remains largely unaddressed. Existing YOLO-based approaches for grape leaf detection, such as those by Yang et al.\cite{yang2024detection}, Chen et al.\cite{chen2024yolov8}, and Ji et al.\cite{ji2025research}, have focused on detection accuracy in isolation, without considering the subsequent classification stage or the combined computational budget of a two-stage detection-classification pipeline. The challenge of aggregating detection and classification results from multiple leaves within a single frame—essential for robust diagnosis in field conditions where image quality varies and multiple leaves are typically visible—has received minimal attention in the literature. Zhou and Yu\cite{zhou2025lightweight} addressed boundary detection in complex scenes but did not extend their work to result aggregation or wireless transmission of aggregated results.

%Para 7: Model Quantization Gap
Model quantization and optimization specifically for microcontroller deployment represent another critical gap. While Grujev et al.\cite{grujev2025efficient} demonstrated Arduino-based deployment for single-stage classification of apple diseases, and Amirkhanova et al.\cite{amirkhanova2026lightweight} explored ESP32-based secure data transmission pipelines, neither addressed the computational challenges of two-stage object detection and classification pipelines. The unique requirements of grape leaf disease detection—including the need for leaf localization in complex vineyard scenes followed by high-accuracy multi-class classification exceeding 99\%—demand specialized optimization strategies. Specifically, the optimization of YOLO models for ESP32-class microcontrollers, particularly the newer ESP32-S3 variant with AI acceleration capabilities, remains an open research question. Similarly, the quantization of MobileNetV2 classifiers to INT8 precision while maintaining classification accuracy above 99\% for four-class grape disease detection has not been systematically investigated in the context of microcontroller deployment with memory constraints below 8MB PSRAM.

%Para 8: Power Management Gap
Power management strategies for duty-cycled operation of edge AI systems in LoRaWAN networks represent an additional operational gap. While existing LoRaWAN implementations such as those by Hnatiuc et al.\cite{hnatiuc2023intelligent} and Almufareh et al.\cite{almufareh2024intelligent} recognize the necessity of deep sleep modes for long-term battery operation, the optimization of capture-inference-transmit cycles specifically for edge AI disease detection has not been comprehensively addressed. The trade-offs between sampling frequency, inference latency, power consumption, and disease detection timeliness require systematic investigation to achieve practical deployment lifetimes exceeding one growing season. Furthermore, the design of LoRaWAN payload formats that efficiently encode disease detection results—including class predictions, confidence scores, and spatial quality metrics—while respecting strict payload size constraints, remains underspecified in existing literature.


%Para 9: Summary & Thesis Contribution
In summary, the research gap addressed by this thesis is the absence of integrated, end-to-end edge AI systems that perform real-time grape leaf disease detection on microcontroller-class devices within LoRaWAN-based wireless sensor networks. Existing literature lacks systems that simultaneously address five critical requirements: (1) deployment of complete object detection and classification pipelines on ESP32-class microcontrollers with memory constraints below 8MB; (2) achievement of real-time inference within power budgets compatible with battery-powered, long-term deployment; (3) implementation of robust result aggregation from multiple detected leaves per frame; (4) formatting and transmission of detection results within LoRaWAN payload constraints; and (5) demonstration of feasibility through complete system implementation and validation. This thesis addresses these gaps through the development and deployment of a four-stage pipeline architecture integrating image capture (OV3660 camera, VGA resolution, JPEG compression), YOLO-based leaf detection (YOLO11n quantized to 478KB INT8, achieving 56.4\% mAP@50), MobileNetV2-based disease classification (quantized to 2.73MB INT8, achieving 99.8\%$\pm$0.05\% accuracy across four disease classes), weighted result aggregation from up to 15 detected leaves, and LoRaWAN transmission on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller platform operating in duty-cycled mode with deep sleep power management.

Early WSN deployments focused on soil moisture sensing for irrigation scheduling~\cite{gutierrez2014automated}, demonstrating that strategically placed sensors could reduce water consumption by 20-40\% compared to uniform irrigation while maintaining or improving crop yields.

Matese et al.~\cite{matese2015technology} deployed a multi-sensor platform measuring temperature, humidity, and leaf wetness at 15-minute intervals across a 5-hectare vineyard, revealing temperature variations of up to 4°C between different row orientations and topographic positions. These microclimate differences significantly affect disease pressure: Downy Mildew infections require specific combinations of temperature (15-25°C), humidity (>80\%), and leaf wetness duration (>4 hours)~\cite{caffi2010mechanistic}, conditions that may occur in some vineyard zones but not others. The authors demonstrated that zone-specific fungicide applications guided by WSN data reduced pesticide usage by 35\% while maintaining equivalent disease control compared to uniform spraying.

% However, environmental sensor networks provide only indirect indicators of plant health. Burrell et al.~\cite{burrell2004vineyard} found that while their 65-node temperature/humidity network successfully predicted disease-favorable conditions with 87\% accuracy, actual disease incidence varied significantly based on cultivar susceptibility, canopy density, and prior infections—factors not captured by environmental measurements alone. The authors concluded that "environmental monitoring is necessary but not sufficient for precision disease management," motivating the integration of direct plant health sensing.

% More recent work has expanded sensor suites to include soil moisture, solar radiation, and wind speed, creating comprehensive environmental profiles. Montoya et al.~\cite{montoya2020vineyard} deployed a 40-node LoRaWAN network across 80 hectares of vineyards, transmitting 12-byte telemetry packets every 20 minutes for 18 months. Their system achieved 99.2\% packet delivery rate and 14-month average battery lifetime using 2×AA lithium batteries, demonstrating the viability of long-term autonomous operation. However, the system lacked visual sensing capabilities, relying entirely on environmental proxies for plant stress detection. The economic analysis revealed that while the WSN reduced water and pesticide costs by 22\%, manual scouting for disease detection still consumed 40\% of labor hours during growing season.

Sigfox provided the lowest power consumption but severely constrained payload sizes (12 bytes) and message quotas (140 uplinks per day).

% Popovic et al.~\cite{popovic2017experiences} reported practical experiences deploying LoRaWAN for agricultural monitoring across 120 hectares, validating theoretical range predictions but identifying several deployment challenges. Gateway placement significantly affected coverage: elevated positions (10-15m above ground) improved range by 40-60\% compared to ground-level installations, while terrain features (hills, tree lines) created shadow zones where packet error rates exceeded 20\%. The authors recommended 2-3 gateways per 100 hectares in complex terrain to ensure redundant coverage. Critically, they found that LoRaWAN's maximum payload of 51-222 bytes (depending on spreading factor) was well-suited for environmental telemetry but prohibitive for image transmission, limiting applications to scalar sensors.

% Trevathan et al.~\cite{trevathan2021iot} surveyed IoT deployments in agriculture, identifying LoRaWAN as the dominant LPWAN choice (64\% of surveyed systems) due to its open specification, mature ecosystem of modules and gateways, and active community support. However, they noted a persistent gap: "visual inspection remains predominantly manual despite decades of research in automated crop monitoring," attributing this to the bandwidth-power constraints that prevent image transmission over LPWANs. The authors called for "intelligent edge processing that extracts semantic information from images before transmission," foreshadowing the edge AI approaches central to this thesis.

% \section{Edge AI on Resource-Constrained Devices}
% \label{sec:edge_ai_deployment}

% Edge AI—deploying neural network inference on sensors and embedded devices rather than cloud servers—has gained attention as a solution to bandwidth, latency, and privacy challenges in IoT applications~\cite{edge_ai_survey}. The fundamental motivation is bandwidth efficiency: transmitting inference results (classification labels, bounding boxes) requires orders of magnitude less data than raw sensor streams (images, audio), enabling AI-powered applications on low-bandwidth networks.

% Early edge AI work targeted mobile phones and embedded Linux platforms (Raspberry Pi, NVIDIA Jetson)~\cite{cai2019once, lane2016deepx}. Cai et al.~\cite{cai2019once} demonstrated on-device training and inference on smartphones, while Lane et al.~\cite{lane2016deepx} showed that model compression (pruning, quantization) could reduce memory and computation by 10-100× with <1\% accuracy loss. However, these platforms consume 1-10W, limiting battery lifetime to hours or days—insufficient for agricultural sensor nodes requiring months of autonomous operation.

% Microcontroller-class edge AI represents a more recent development. ARM's announcement of Cortex-M55 CPU with Helium vector extensions and Ethos-U55 neural processing unit (2019) enabled CNN inference at 1-10 FPS on milliwatt-scale platforms~\cite{mehonic2022brain}. Subsequent microcontrollers from STMicroelectronics (STM32H7), NXP (i.MX RT1060), and Espressif (ESP32-S3) incorporated similar hardware acceleration, creating an ecosystem of AI-capable MCUs consuming 100-300mW during inference and <100$\mu$W in sleep modes.

% TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers~\cite{david2021tensorflow} and competitor frameworks (ARM CMSIS-NN, Espressif ESP-DL) provide optimized kernels for INT8 and INT16 inference on MCU-class processors. David et al.~\cite{david2021tensorflow} demonstrated keyword spotting, gesture recognition, and anomaly detection on Cortex-M4F microcontrollers, achieving 5-15 FPS for small models (<500KB). However, these applications used relatively simple networks (MobileNet-v1 with width multiplier 0.25); larger models required for agricultural disease classification (MobileNet-v2, ResNet-18) challenged memory constraints.

% Quantization-aware training (QAT) and post-training quantization (PTQ) reduce model precision from 32-bit floating-point to 8-bit integer, compressing models 4× and accelerating inference 2-4× on hardware with INT8 SIMD instructions~\cite{jacob2018quantization}. Krishnamoorthi~\cite{krishnamoorthi2018quantize} showed that QAT maintains accuracy within 1-2\% of full-precision baselines for ImageNet classification, while PTQ often degrades accuracy by 3-5\% for complex models. For plant disease classification, Chen et al.~\cite{chen2021mobile} found that INT8 quantization of MobileNet-v2 preserved 97.8\% accuracy compared to 98.5\% for FP32, validating quantization as a practical compression technique.

% Agricultural edge AI deployments remain limited. Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú~\cite{kamilaris2018deep} surveyed deep learning in agriculture, identifying only 12\% of papers that addressed embedded deployment, and of those, most targeted Raspberry Pi or Jetson rather than microcontrollers. Li et al.~\cite{li2020edge} deployed MobileNet-v2 on NVIDIA Jetson Nano for cucumber disease detection, achieving 96.5\% accuracy at 12 FPS but consuming 5W—requiring wall power rather than battery operation. To the author's knowledge, no prior work has demonstrated end-to-end leaf disease detection (object detection + classification) on microcontroller-class platforms (ESP32, STM32, etc.) with battery-powered autonomous operation.
